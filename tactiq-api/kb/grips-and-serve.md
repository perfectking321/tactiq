# Grips and the serve

## Forehand grip
The basic forehand grip is often described as "shaking hands" with the racket handle, with the racket face
perpendicular to the floor. The V formed by the thumb and index finger sits on the narrow upper bevel of the
handle. The grip should be relaxed between shots; squeezing tightly all the time slows the wrist and forearm
and tires the forearm muscles. Tighten the grip only at the moment of impact for power shots.

## Backhand grip
For many backhand shots the thumb is moved onto the broad back bevel of the handle (the "thumb grip"), which
gives a lever to push the racket through. Small grip changes between forehand and backhand are a normal part
of play. Beginners commonly use a "panhandle" grip (racket face flat like a frying pan) for everything; it is
fine at the net for quick pushes but limits power and angle elsewhere.

## Rules of the serve
Under the BWF Laws, the whole shuttle must be below a fixed height of 1.15 m from the court surface at the
moment of impact, the racket must move forward continuously from the start of the serve, and both feet must
stay in contact with the floor and inside the service court until the shuttle is hit. The server must not
delay unduly once ready. Players should check current laws for details as they can change.

## Low (short) serve
The low serve is the standard serve in doubles and is widely used in singles. It aims to pass just over the
net tape and land near the front service line, so the receiver cannot attack it downwards. The backhand
low serve is popular because the short swing is consistent and hides the shot. Key points: a quiet stance,
a short push rather than a swing, and the same preparation for low and flick serves so the receiver cannot
read it.

## High serve and flick serve
The high (long) serve sends the shuttle high and deep to the back of the singles service court. It is mainly
used in singles, especially in women's singles and at beginner level, to push the opponent to the rear. The
flick serve looks like a low serve but is flicked over the receiver's head; it is used as a surprise in
doubles against a receiver who rushes the net. A predictable flick is easily smashed, so it must be disguised
and used sparingly.
