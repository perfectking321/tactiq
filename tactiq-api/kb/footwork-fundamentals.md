# Footwork fundamentals

## Why footwork matters in badminton
Good footwork lets a player reach the shuttle early, in balance, and with time to choose a shot. Most
amateur players lose rallies not because their strokes are poor but because they arrive late and off
balance, which forces weak lifts and errors. Efficient movement also reduces energy cost over a long match
and lowers stress on the knees and ankles, because a balanced player does not have to brake hard with a
twisted leg. Footwork should be trained as a skill in its own right, not only as fitness.

## Ready position and base
Between shots the player returns to a **base** position, usually slightly behind the centre of the court in
singles. In the ready position the feet are about shoulder-width apart, knees slightly bent, weight on the
balls of the feet, racket up in front of the body. A low, relaxed stance lets the player push off in any
direction. Standing upright with straight legs or flat feet slows the first step. In singles the base shifts
slightly depending on the shot just played (for example a little further back after a high clear).

## The split step
The split step is a small hop or "unweighting" timed just as the opponent hits the shuttle. The player
lands with feet slightly wider than the shoulders and knees flexed, which pre-loads the leg muscles so the
first push-off is explosive. Timing is the key: too early and the player is already landed and static, too
late and they are still in the air when they need to move. Practise by calling "hit" as a partner strikes
and landing on the call. A good split step is one of the quickest ways for an intermediate player to look
faster without becoming fitter.

## Moving to the six corners
Court coverage is often practised as movement to six points: two front corners (forehand and backhand net),
two side mid-court points, and two rear corners. Common movement patterns are:
- **Chasse (side-step):** feet slide without crossing; good for short, controlled distances.
- **Cross-step / running steps:** used to cover longer distances quickly, for example to the rear court.
- **Lunge:** the final step to the net or to a wide mid-court shot; the racket-side leg leads for most shots.
- **Scissor kick / jump:** used at the rear court on attacking overhead shots so the player can land and
  recover forward quickly.
Every movement ends with a **recovery** back towards base, ideally starting as the shot is played.

## Lunging safely
In a correct lunge the front foot points roughly towards the shuttle, the heel lands first and rolls to the
whole foot, and the knee tracks in line with the toes rather than collapsing inward. The back leg stays
behind for balance and helps push back. Overreaching (lunging so far that the trunk falls forward) puts
heavy load on the front knee and lower back. Research on badminton injuries repeatedly links poor lunging
and landing mechanics to knee and ankle problems, so technique and leg strength both matter.

## Footwork drills
- **Shadow footwork:** moving to the six corners without a shuttle, following a coach's pointing or a random
  call order. Start slowly with perfect form, then add speed.
- **Multi-feed / multi-shuttle drills:** a feeder sends shuttles rapidly to different corners; very effective
  but demanding, so keep sets short (20-40 seconds) with full recovery.
- **Two-corner drills:** for example front-backhand to rear-forehand, to build specific patterns.
Quality first: tired, sloppy footwork repeated many times teaches bad habits.
