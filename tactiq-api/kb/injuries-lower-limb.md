# Common lower-limb injuries in badminton

Important: this is general education, not a diagnosis. Any significant, worsening or persistent pain should
be assessed by a physiotherapist or doctor.

## Where badminton injuries happen
Reviews of badminton injuries consistently report that the **lower limb** is the most commonly injured region,
especially the knee and ankle, followed by the shoulder and the lower back. Many injuries are overuse injuries
that build up gradually from repeated lunging, jumping and landing, rather than single accidents. Risk factors
discussed in the research include high training volume, sudden increases in load, previous injury, and poor
lunging and landing mechanics.

## Ankle sprain
Ankle sprains usually happen when landing on the outside of the foot, for example landing on an opponent's or
partner's foot or after a jump smash. Typical signs are pain and swelling on the outer ankle. Early care for
mild sprains follows the PEACE approach: **P**rotect for a few days, **E**levate, **A**void anti-inflammatory
overuse early on, **C**ompress, **E**ducate yourself; followed by LOVE: gradual **L**oad, **O**ptimism,
**V**ascularisation (pain-free cardio), **E**xercise (balance and strength). **Seek medical assessment** if the
player cannot bear weight for four steps, there is tenderness on the ankle bones, severe swelling or deformity,
because a fracture must be ruled out. Balance training (single-leg standing, wobble board) reduces the chance
of re-spraining.

## Knee pain and patellar tendinopathy
Pain at the front of the knee, just below the kneecap, that worsens with jumping, lunging or stairs may be
**patellar tendinopathy** ("jumper's knee"), an overuse problem of the tendon. It usually responds to reducing
jumping and deep lunging volume temporarily and progressive strengthening (isometric holds, then slow heavy
squats, then plyometrics) guided by a physiotherapist. Pain that is sharp, comes with locking, giving way, or
significant swelling after a twisting injury may indicate ligament or meniscus damage and needs prompt
assessment.

## Achilles tendon and calf
Gradual pain and stiffness in the Achilles tendon, worse in the morning or at the start of play, suggests
**Achilles tendinopathy**; progressive calf-raise strengthening is the usual core treatment. A sudden sharp pain
at the back of the ankle, often described as feeling kicked, with weakness pushing off, can be an **Achilles
rupture**, which is an emergency for medical assessment. Calf muscle strains also happen during sudden
push-offs; warm up the calves properly.

## Heel pain (plantar fasciitis)
Pain under the heel with the first steps in the morning is commonly plantar fasciopathy. Calf and foot
strengthening, supportive court shoes and managing training volume usually help. Persistent cases should be
reviewed by a professional.
