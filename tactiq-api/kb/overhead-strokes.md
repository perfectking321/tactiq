# Overhead strokes: clear, drop and smash

## Common preparation for overhead shots
All good overhead shots start from the same preparation: move behind the shuttle early, turn sideways with
the non-racket shoulder towards the net, raise the non-racket arm to track the shuttle, and bring the racket
up behind the head with the elbow high. Hitting the shuttle in front of the body and at full reach gives the
best angle and power. Using the same preparation for clear, drop and smash is what gives **deception**: the
opponent cannot tell which shot is coming.

## The clear
The clear sends the shuttle high and deep to the opponent's back court. An **attacking clear** is flatter and
faster, aiming to get behind an opponent who is too far forward. A **defensive clear** is higher, giving the
player time to recover to base. Power comes from a chain: leg drive, hip and trunk rotation, shoulder, elbow
extension and forearm rotation (pronation), finishing with a relaxed follow-through. Weak clears that land in
mid-court are a common beginner problem and are usually caused by hitting the shuttle too far behind the
body or using only the arm.

## The drop shot
The drop shot is played from the rear court to land close to the net in the opponent's front court. A **slow
drop** falls just over the net; a **fast (or sliced) drop** travels quicker and lands nearer the service line,
giving the opponent less time. The drop is effective when it looks like a smash or clear until the last
moment. A drop that floats high over the net invites a net kill, so keep the trajectory falling as it crosses
the net.

## The smash
The smash is the main attacking shot: a steep, powerful downward hit. It is most effective from a position
behind the shuttle with the contact point in front of the body. Steepness and placement (to the body, the
hip on the racket side, or the sidelines) often matter more than raw speed. Jump smashes add angle but cost a
lot of energy and load the landing leg; amateur players should use them selectively. After smashing, move
forward quickly because the most likely reply is a block to the net.

## Common overhead faults
- Letting the shuttle drop too low or behind the head, which kills both power and angle.
- Facing the net square-on instead of turning sideways.
- Gripping the racket tightly throughout the swing.
- Not recovering after the shot, especially after a smash.
Video recording a few overhead shots from the side is an easy way to spot these faults.
