# Sleep, recovery and overtraining

## Sleep is the most important recovery tool
Training creates the stimulus; recovery is when the body adapts. Adults are recommended to get at least 7 hours
of sleep per night, and teenagers typically need about 8-10 hours. Short or poor sleep is linked with slower
reaction times, worse decision-making, lower mood and a higher risk of injury and illness. For student athletes,
late-night studying and screen use are common reasons for poor sleep.

## Practical sleep habits
- Keep a regular sleep and wake time, including weekends where possible.
- Reduce screens and bright light for 30-60 minutes before bed; keep the room dark, quiet and cool.
- Avoid caffeine in the late afternoon and evening.
- After late evening matches, use a short wind-down routine (shower, light snack, reading) because the body
  stays alert after intense play.
- A short nap of 20-30 minutes in the early afternoon can help when night sleep was short.

## Active recovery and easy days
Light activity (easy cycling, walking, swimming, gentle mobility) on the day after a hard session can help
players feel less stiff. Foam rolling and stretching can make players feel better, although evidence that they
speed physical recovery is mixed. The most effective recovery tools remain sleep, good nutrition, hydration and
sensible scheduling of hard and easy days.

## Muscle soreness
Delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS) usually peaks 24-72 hours after new or unusually hard exercise and settles
by itself. It feels like general muscle stiffness and tenderness, unlike sharp pain in one specific spot, joint
pain, or swelling, which may indicate an injury.

## Warning signs of overtraining or under-recovery
Watch for several of these together over more than a few days: persistently high soreness, low energy, poor
sleep, irritability or low mood, falling performance despite training, frequent colds, and loss of appetite.
In the TactiQ daily check-in these appear as rising soreness and stress scores with falling energy and sleep.
The response is to reduce training load for several days, prioritise sleep and food, and speak to a coach or
doctor if it does not improve.
